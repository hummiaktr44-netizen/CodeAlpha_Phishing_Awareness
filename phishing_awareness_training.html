What is phishing?

Phishing is a cyber attack where someone pretends to be a trusted person or company to trick you into giving away passwords, money, or personal data, or into installing malware. It usually arrives by email, but also by SMS (smishing), phone calls (vishing), and chat apps.

Why it works: attackers target people, not machines. A message that looks normal and creates pressure makes people act before they think.
TypeDescription
Mass phishingSame fake message sent to thousands of people.
Spear phishingPersonalised message aimed at one person, using their name, job or colleagues.
WhalingSpear phishing aimed at executives or finance staff.
Smishing / VishingPhishing by text message or phone call.

How to recognize a phishing email

From: PayPal Support <security@paypa1-help.com>
Subject: URGENT: Your account will be closed in 24 hours!

Dear Customer,

We noticed unusual activity on your account. Please to verify your informations immediately to avoid suspension.

Click here to verify: www.paypal.com/verify

Attachment: Account_Details.zip

Tap a highlight to see the explanation.

Checklist

  • Check the sender's real email address, not just the display name.
  • Look for urgency, threats, or too-good-to-be-true offers.
  • Hover over links to preview the real destination.
  • Be suspicious of unexpected attachments and requests for passwords or payment.
  • Check spelling, grammar and generic greetings.

How to recognize fake websites

A fake site copies the look of a real one. The address bar is where it gives itself away. Compare these made-up examples for a bank at examplebank.com:

URLVerdictWhy
https://login.examplebank.comGenuineThe domain (the part just before .com) is examplebank.com.
https://examplebank.com.secure-login.netFakeThe real domain is secure-login.net. The bank name is just a subdomain.
https://examp1ebank.comFakeLook-alike character (1 instead of l), called typosquatting.
https://examplebank.com@verify.netFakeEverything before the @ is ignored. You are going to verify.net.
  • A padlock and HTTPS only mean the connection is encrypted. Phishing sites can have them too.
  • Type the address yourself or use a saved bookmark instead of clicking links in messages.
  • Be careful with login pages that ask for extra data such as card PIN or full ID number.
  • Watch for pop-ups, wrong logos, and pages that load only after you click a message.

Social engineering tactics

Social engineering means manipulating people psychologically so they hand over access or information. Phishing is one form of it.

TacticHow it worksExample
Urgency / fearForces a fast decision."Your account is locked. Act now."
AuthorityPretends to be a boss, bank, or police."This is the CEO. Send gift cards today."
PretextingInvents a believable story.Caller says they are IT support and need your password.
BaitingOffers something tempting.Free prize, or a USB drive left in a car park.
Reward / greedPromises money or gifts."You won a lottery. Pay the fee to claim."
ImpersonationUses a real person's name or hacked account.A colleague's account asking you to open a shared file.

Best practices to avoid becoming a victim

  • Stop and think. Pressure is a warning sign. Take a moment.
  • Verify separately. Contact the company through its official website or phone number, not the message.
  • Use multi-factor authentication (MFA) or passkeys, so a stolen password alone is not enough.
  • Use a password manager. It will not autofill on a fake domain, which is a useful alarm.
  • Never share passwords, OTP codes, or card PINs with anyone.
  • Keep software updated and use antivirus and email spam filters.
  • Report it. Tell your IT or security team, mark the email as phishing, and delete it.
  • If you clicked: disconnect from the network if you opened a file, change your passwords, enable MFA, and report immediately.

Real-world examples

Fake invoices to Google and Facebook (2013 to 2015)

A Lithuanian man posed as a hardware supplier and sent convincing fake invoices. Google and Facebook together paid out more than $100 million before it was discovered. Lesson: verify payment requests through a second channel.

US election campaign email hack (2016)

A fake "security alert" email with a link to a spoofed Google login page led to the theft of a campaign chairman's emails. Lesson: urgent security alerts are a classic trap. Go to the real site directly.

Twitter employee phone phishing (2020)

Attackers phoned Twitter staff pretending to be internal IT, gained access to internal tools, and took over high-profile accounts. Lesson: voice calls can be social engineering too.
